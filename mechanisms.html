Reference

Antibiotic mechanisms of action

Where each antibiotic class acts on the bacterial cell, whether it kills or stops growth, and how bacteria become resistant. Tap a numbered target to see the details and the drugs on this site.

Checked November 2026Main sources: Kapoor 2017; Blair 2015

Key points

  • Synergy: drugs acting at different steps can work better together: a β-lactam opens the wall so more aminoglycoside gets in (enterococcal endocarditis), and sulfamethoxazole with trimethoprim blocks two steps of folate synthesis.
  • Antagonism: bacteriostatic drugs can blunt β-lactams, which need actively growing cells to kill.
  • Toxin suppression: protein synthesis inhibitors (clindamycin, linezolid) stop toxin production, so they are added in toxic shock and necrotising fasciitis.
  • Cidal or static: the distinction matters most when host defences are poor: endocarditis, meningitis and neutropenia.
  • Same target, cross-resistance: erm methylation affects macrolides and clindamycin together, and one rpoB mutation removes rifampicin entirely, so rifampicin and fusidic acid are combined for systemic use.
