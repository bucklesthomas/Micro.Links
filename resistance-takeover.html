Resistance takeover

Watch a population of bacteria under treatment. Blue cells are susceptible, red cells are resistant. Change the dose, course length and missed doses to see when the infection clears, relapses, or resistant bacteria take over.

Checked November 2026A simplified teaching model

Teaching points

  • Resistance is selected, not caused, by antibiotics. Resistant cells are usually there already (or appear by mutation); the antibiotic removes their competitors.
  • The mutant selection window is the range between the MIC of the susceptible population and the level needed to stop resistant mutants (the mutant prevention concentration). Time spent in it favours resistance.
  • Adequate dosing matters: under-dosing and long gaps between doses keep levels in the window.
  • Stopping too early risks relapse, but most courses can be short: once the bacteria are cleared, extra days add side effects and resistance in the normal flora without benefit.
  • Combination therapy (for example in TB, or rifampicin with another drug) prevents a single mutation from being enough.
  • Without antibiotic pressure resistant strains often grow slightly slower (a fitness cost), which is one reason reducing antibiotic use helps.

A simplified model for teaching: each square stands for a clump of millions of bacteria, the drug has a 3-hour half-life given every 8 hours, and resistant cells need 32 times the level to be killed. Real infections also depend on the site, the immune response, source control and the drug.
