Reference

PK/PD targets explained

How antibiotics kill depends on how long the concentration stays above the MIC, how high the peak gets, or the total exposure over the day. The target decides the best way to dose.

Checked November 2026Main sources: Craig 1998; Roberts 2014; Rybak 2020

Time-dependent

fT>MIC

The percentage of the dosing interval that the free (unbound) drug concentration stays above the MIC. Higher peaks add little once the concentration is a few times the MIC.

Drugs
Penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems, aztreonam
Targets
About 40% for carbapenems, 50% for penicillins, 60–70% for cephalosporins; aim for 100% in critical illness
Dosing
Dose more often, or give extended (3-hour) or continuous infusions, rather than bigger doses

Concentration-dependent

Cmax/MIC

The peak concentration divided by the MIC. Killing is faster the higher the peak, and a long post-antibiotic effect suppresses regrowth after levels fall.

Drugs
Aminoglycosides (gentamicin, amikacin, tobramycin)
Target
Cmax/MIC 8–10
Dosing
One large dose once a day: a high peak, then a drug-free period that lowers kidney and ear toxicity (trough-guided)

Exposure-dependent

AUC/MIC

The area under the concentration–time curve over 24 hours divided by the MIC: the total exposure over the day, however it is split.

Drugs
Vancomycin, fluoroquinolones, linezolid, macrolides, tetracyclines, daptomycin
Targets
Vancomycin AUC24 400–600 (MIC 1); levofloxacin fAUC/MIC 30–50 for pneumococcus, 100–125 for Gram-negatives; linezolid 80–120
Dosing
The total daily dose matters most; use AUC-guided monitoring for vancomycin

Why each class kills the way it does

The PK/PD target follows from what the drug does inside the bacterium: whether it needs the cell to be growing, whether more drug means more target hit, and how quickly the bacterium recovers once the drug has gone.

Time-dependent · cell wall

β-lactams

They bind the penicillin-binding proteins (transpeptidases) that cross-link new peptidoglycan as the cell grows and divides. The cell’s own autolysins keep cutting the wall to make room, new cross-links fail, and the cell bursts, often at the dividing septum.

Why time
The PBPs are saturated at about 4 times the MIC, so a higher peak adds little. What matters is keeping them blocked through every round of growth, so time above the MIC decides the effect
So
Little killing of cells that are not growing: stationary phase, biofilm, abscess pus and very high inocula (the Eagle and inoculum effects). Gram-negatives make new PBPs quickly, so there is little post-antibiotic effect (carbapenems excepted)
Enterococci
Lack the autolysis step (tolerance): ampicillin alone only inhibits growth, which is why endocarditis needs a second drug

AUC-dependent · cell wall

Glycopeptides

Vancomycin and teicoplanin bind the D-Ala-D-Ala end of the wall building blocks (lipid II) outside the membrane, so they cannot be added to the wall. They are large molecules that cannot cross the Gram-negative outer membrane.

Why AUC
Killing is slow and needs growing cells, with a moderate post-antibiotic effect against Gram-positives, so the total exposure over the day (AUC/MIC 400–600) predicts the result
So
Slower killing than flucloxacillin or cefazolin for MSSA; poor in thick vegetations and biofilm

Concentration-dependent · ribosome

Aminoglycosides

They are pulled across the cell membrane by the proton-motive force, faster the higher the outside concentration. On the 30S ribosome they cause misreading; faulty proteins insert into the membrane and let in more drug, so killing speeds up as it goes.

Why peak
Uptake, and so killing, rises with concentration. Ribosomes take hours to recover, giving a long post-antibiotic effect. After a dose, uptake is switched down for a while (adaptive resistance), which suits one large daily dose
So
Kill growing and non-growing cells, but need oxygen and a normal pH: inactive against anaerobes and poor in abscesses. Damage to the wall by a β-lactam lets more in (synergy in endocarditis)

Concentration and AUC · DNA

Fluoroquinolones

They trap DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV on the DNA after they have cut it. When the replication machinery runs into these trapped complexes, the DNA breaks on both strands.

Why peak and AUC
More drug traps more complexes, so killing rises with concentration; AUC/MIC (about 125 for Gram-negatives) is the usual target, with a post-antibiotic effect
So
Kill best when DNA is being copied, but also some non-dividing cells. Low levels sit in the mutant selection window, so under-dosing breeds resistance

Concentration-dependent · membrane

Daptomycin and polymyxins

Daptomycin, with calcium, inserts into the Gram-positive cell membrane and depolarises it. Polymyxins bind lipid A of Gram-negative LPS, push out the calcium and magnesium that hold it together, and break both membranes.

Why peak
Membrane damage is direct and rises with concentration, so killing is fast and does not need growth (Cmax/MIC or AUC/MIC)
So
Useful in biofilm and high inocula. Daptomycin is inactivated by lung surfactant, so it fails in pneumonia

AUC-dependent · ribosome

Protein synthesis inhibitors

Macrolides, tetracyclines, clindamycin and linezolid bind the ribosome reversibly and stop protein being made. The bacteria stop growing while the drug is present and the immune system clears them: mostly bacteriostatic.

Why AUC
The effect depends on how much of the day the ribosomes are blocked, so total exposure (AUC/MIC) predicts the result
So
Stop toxin production even in non-growing bacteria, which is why clindamycin or linezolid is added in necrotising fasciitis and toxic shock. Avoid relying on them alone where you need killing: endocarditis, meningitis, neutropenia

AUC and peak · RNA polymerase

Rifampicin

Blocks bacterial RNA polymerase, so no new RNA is made. This still works in slow-growing and dormant bacteria.

So
Reaches bacteria in biofilm on prosthetic joints and valves, and the slowly dividing persisters in TB. A single mutation in rpoB gives resistance, so it is never used alone. Higher doses are being studied because killing rises with exposure

Concentration-dependent · DNA

Metronidazole

A prodrug that is only switched on by the low-redox enzymes of anaerobes and some protozoa. The activated drug breaks DNA.

So
Rapid, concentration-dependent killing of anaerobes only; no activity against aerobes because they cannot activate it

Things that move the curve

  • Protein binding: only free drug is active. For highly bound drugs (flucloxacillin, ceftriaxone, ertapenem, teicoplanin) a total level can look adequate while the free level is below the MIC, especially when albumin is low.
  • Critical illness: augmented renal clearance and a larger volume of distribution lower β-lactam levels; extended or continuous infusion and TDM help. Kidney injury does the opposite.
  • MIC: a higher MIC needs more exposure for every target. Use the EUCAST breakpoints and the isolate’s MIC, not just “S”.
  • Post-antibiotic effect: aminoglycosides and fluoroquinolones keep suppressing Gram-negative growth after levels fall below the MIC; β-lactams have little effect against Gram-negatives (except carbapenems).
  • Resistance: levels between the MIC and the mutant prevention concentration (the mutant selection window) favour resistant mutants; short, high exposures and combination therapy narrow it.
